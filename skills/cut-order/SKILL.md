# Cut Order

> "For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the
> cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?" — Luke 14:28

## The move

Before anyone builds, publish two lists:

**(a) The critical path** — the things without which the deliverable fails. The never-cut
list.

**(b) The order in which everything else gets cut** — first to go, second, third.

Not when you are behind. At the start.

## Why the ordering has to be fixed in advance

At 4am the cut decision is made by whoever is most attached to their own piece, under
pressure, defending. **Decided at hour zero it costs nothing and binds everyone equally —
including the person who wrote it.**

Fatigue is only half of it. Deciding late means deciding among people who now have sunk work
in the thing being cut. Deciding early means deciding among people who have nothing yet, and
the argument is about the deliverable instead of about them.

## What it actually buys — measured

Proven across eight parallel agents in one night, building to a morning deadline:

- **The riskiest, most expensive item was pre-designated to degrade first.** So when the
  reference material it was meant to imitate turned out to be far simpler than assumed, the
  top of the ladder **degraded into the look the team wanted** rather than into a hole. A
  ladder written in advance converts a scope loss into a decision that was already made.
- **Two agents applied the ladder to their own spheres, unasked** — declining work they could
  see how to do better. That is the hardest direction to apply a cut rule in, and it only
  happens when the rule pre-dates the attachment.
- **The never-cut list gave a short, checkable answer to "can this wait?"** every time it came
  up, which is most of what a deadline actually costs in interruptions.

## Companion rules that made it hold

- **Sequence the blockers for the loaded worker.** When three urgent things converge on one
  person, do not ask whether it is too much. Hand them an order.
- **Put an integration checkpoint early and deliberately thin.** One verb, one view, both real
  sides, before either side is complete. Nights die at integration — see
  [own-the-seam](../own-the-seam/SKILL.md) for why the join is the part nobody is watching.
- **Watch for serial chains that end in a never-cut item.** A protected feature sitting behind
  three sequential dependencies is not protected by being on the list. Break the chain with a
  thin slice now and fill it in later.

## Writing one (ten minutes)

1. Name the deliverable in one sentence, and name the moment it gets judged.
2. **Never-cut:** list what makes it *this* deliverable rather than a different one. Keep it
   short enough to recite from memory — it has to be usable as an answer, not consulted as a
   document.
3. **Ladder:** everything else, ordered first-cut to last. Put the riskiest and most expensive
   item at the top on purpose: the item most likely to be cut is exactly the one where a
   planned degradation beats an unplanned hole.
4. For each rung, say what the degraded version *is*. "Cut the animated map" is a hole;
   "fall back to the static map" is a design.
5. Publish it where the work happens, before the work starts, and say out loud that it binds
   you too. A ladder its author is exempt from is not a ladder.
